\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reading and steering directions in representations.}
Representation engineering extracts a direction for a concept by contrasting paired
stimuli, then validates it by injecting it back and watching behaviour
change~\cite{zou2023repe}. The recipe has been applied to honesty, refusal and
sentiment in language models, and the injection step is what separates a direction
that \emph{describes} a state from one that \emph{drives} it. We adopt both halves:
the paired-difference reading and the injection control, including the operator forms
and the caution that a perturbation applied early is attenuated downstream. What we
add is the discipline of reporting the injection result when it comes out null---in
our setting the projection reads well and drives poorly, and we say so.

\paragraph{Robustness to appearance in driving.}
Corruption benchmarks perturb driving inputs with weather, blur and noise and report
the drop in a task metric~\cite{kong2023robodepth}. That tells you \emph{that} a policy
degrades; it does not say whether the degradation is safe or unsafe, nor what moved
inside. Our question is narrower and, we think, more diagnostic: does an appearance
change move the representation along the same axis the policy uses to represent
hazards, and in which direction? A drop in displacement error is compatible with both
excessive caution and excessive speed; the sign of $\Delta b$ is not.

\paragraph{Closed-loop and open-loop scoring.}
nuScenes~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} and the nuPlan-derived NAVSIM
protocol~\cite{dauner2024navsim} score a policy against logged human behaviour, the
latter through a non-reactive simulation with map-based compliance terms. Both are
displacement- or compliance-centric, and a policy that never reacts to a hazard can
score well while staying close to the recorded trajectory. We use the official score
only as an external reference for one pre-registered test (\Cref{tab:negative}); the
rest of our evidence does not depend on it.

\paragraph{Counterfactual object removal.}
Removing an object and measuring the change in the plan is the standard way to ask
whether a policy is responding to it. The measurement is more fragile than it looks:
we find that filling the removed region with noise---rather than with the colour of
the surface behind it---inflates the measured response four- to eight-fold and
contaminates the control arm (\Cref{sec:controls}). We report the fill rule explicitly
for this reason.

\paragraph{Pre-registration in empirical ML.}
Pre-specifying hypotheses and decision rules is standard in the sciences and rare in
our field. It earned its place here: our leading hypothesis showed a perfect rank
correlation on the four policies that generated it, and $\rho = +0.03$ once two more
were added. Without the timestamped prediction we would have reported the first
number.
