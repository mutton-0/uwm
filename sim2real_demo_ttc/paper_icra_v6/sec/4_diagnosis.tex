\subsection{The report}
\Cref{tab:report} and \cref{fig:reach}b give the six profiles.

\paragraph{Exposure}
The six policies do not even agree on how far to plan, and this alone will turn out to explain more of
the collision picture below than perception does: exposure spans a factor of four, from DiffusionDrive
at half the human's distance to SimLingo at more than twice it, with AutoVLA and Alpamayo-1.5 near the
human's own.

\paragraph{Hazard sensitivity}
No policy manages the pedestrian risk. On units that need a reaction, seeing the pedestrian buys
\NumHSlo--\NumHShi{} of safety on a $[-1,1]$ scale. The benefit does not grow with the hazard: its rank
correlation with $a_{\mathrm{req}}$ is negative for \NumScalNeg{} policies, down to \NumScalLo{} for
\NumScalWorst; DiffusionDriveV2 is the only exception (\NumScalHi), and its benefit stays below
$0.15$. Replacing the hazard axis by the time-based $\mathrm{TTC}_0$, whose sign is reversed by
construction, leaves the picture unchanged (\NumScalTtcLo{} to \NumScalTtcHi, against \NumScalLo{} to
\NumScalHi). The benefit is also unreliable in direction:
counting only cases where seeing the pedestrian changes the outcome, it turns a collision into a clean
pass about as often as it turns a pass into a collision, for every policy (DiffusionDrive 9 vs.\ 5,
SimLingo 18 vs.\ 30, the rest at most 5 either way; all McNemar $p\ge0.11$) --- what reads as a safety
margin in \cref{tab:report} is, case by case, close to a coin flip.

\paragraph{Specificity}
A policy that moves when nothing requires it is not merely cautious, it is unpredictable, and
unpredictability is what turns out to travel across a domain shift (\cref{sec:sample}). SimLingo's
plan moves most when nothing requires it ($\SP=\NumSpSL$); the other five stay steadier, at
\NumSpOtherLo--\NumSpOtherHi.

\paragraph{Collisions}
Collisions with the pedestrian's logged future follow how far a policy plans to drive, not what it perceives. At the logged
speed, the visible and removed rates differ by at most \NumCollGap{} points for every policy, and at
8\,m/s by at most \NumCollGapEight. SimLingo, which plans the longest trajectories, collides most. The low-exposure
policies almost never reach the pedestrian. DiffusionDrive is safe because it stays short, AutoVLA and Alpamayo-1.5 cover a human-like distance
without yielding, and SimLingo covers the most ground with the least stable plan.

The check-up only diagnoses; two mechanistic observations on the three BEV planners explain the
diagnosis without altering it. First, the planners' own BEV semantic heads do not decode the
pedestrian: on 520 NAVSIM near-pedestrian scenes the pedestrian channel is no brighter at the
pedestrian than at a mirrored empty location, and never wins the argmax inside a pedestrian's box,
although the pedestrian does leave a steerable trace in the features. Second, the plan does not reach
the pedestrian: plans end 5--9\,m ahead while the median corridor pedestrian stands 20\,m away, and
raising the input speed makes collisions rise identically with and without the pedestrian in view
(\cref{fig:reach}a). The limit is geometric before it is perceptual.
