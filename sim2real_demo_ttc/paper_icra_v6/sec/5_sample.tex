\subsection{Prognosis: What Transfers, and at What Cost}
\label{sec:sample}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/sample_size.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{What a verdict costs.} One frame is 5\,s of near-pedestrian driving. (a)~Spread of
each estimate against sample size with its fitted law, one line per policy. (b)~Driving needed per
verdict, one dot per policy, off-scale values at the axis end.}
\label{fig:sample}

Two questions decide whether a few hundred edited frames can stand in for a rank: which verdicts carry over to the other driving side, and how many frames each verdict needs.

\paragraph{A pre-registered transfer test}
Does a diagnosis made on one driving side describe the same policy on the other? Before computing any
right-hand-drive statistic, we fixed six predictions, P1--P6 in \cref{tab:prereg}, and their decision rules. They were derived from the profile of the 88 Boston
frames, and we tested them on the near-pedestrian units of the 148 Singapore frames
(\cref{tab:prereg}). The registration covered the six policies public at that time. NVIDIA
subsequently released Alpamayo~1.5, which accepts navigation instructions, and we use it in place of
Alpamayo-R1 throughout this paper. Because both driving sides of Alpamayo~1.5 were measured together,
its numbers do not satisfy the registration discipline, so the table is evaluated on the five policies
that do. Retaining the replaced policy changes none of the six verdicts.

\input{tables/tab_prereg}

\paragraph{What transferred}
\NumPass{} of the six predictions hold, and they are the orderings and verdicts. The night-style perturbation
outweighs the pedestrian in every policy. Exposure keeps its order ($\rho=0.90$), and so does specificity, with SimLingo the lowest. SimLingo collides most, with the visible and removed
rates within 3 points of each other.

\paragraph{What did not}
Two predictions fail.
\begin{itemize}
\item Point values drift: only 60\% of the model--exam cells stay inside their Boston interval.
\item In Singapore, DiffusionDrive and DiffusionDriveV2 show a small but non-zero hazard sensitivity
      ($\HS=0.09$ and $0.08$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reading}
A small check-up can therefore report an ordering and a verdict for use in a new domain, but not a
calibrated value.

\paragraph{The standard scores on the same frames}
\input{tables/tab_bench}

These policies are usually compared by a leaderboard rank or an open-loop error. Running those scores
and the check-up on the same six policies (\cref{tab:bench}) lets us ask what each recovers that the
other does not, and which of them predicts behaviour at a site it has not seen.

\paragraph{nuScenes open-loop metrics}
On our 236 near-pedestrian frames, removing the pedestrian from the image changes none of the standard
open-loop numbers: the L2 error by at most \NumMaxdLtwo\,m, the frontal collision rate by at most
\NumMaxdCol{} points and the pedestrian collision rate by at most \NumMaxdPed{} (\cref{tab:bench}
shows the L2 pairs, the two collision rates behave the same way and are omitted). The metric cannot tell whether a policy saw the pedestrian. Its ordering follows how
far a policy's planned distance departs from the human's (rank correlation \NumRhoLtwoExp{} with
$|\text{exposure}-1|$): L2 rewards driving at the human's speed. We exclude rear-end contacts from the
collision count. The logged traffic does not react, so a policy that plans to drive slower than the
human is scored as being hit from behind.

\paragraph{NAVSIM, sliced to near pedestrians}
On all 783 Singapore scenes, AutoVLA, Alpamayo-1.5 and DiffusionDriveV2 score highest. On the
\NumNclose{} scenes with a pedestrian near the ego corridor, the ordering is unrelated to that one
($\rho=\NumRhoAllClose$). The two rank columns of \cref{tab:bench} can be read side by side: the
leaderboard's first (AutoVLA) falls to fifth and its third (DiffusionDriveV2) to last, while its
fourth and fifth (DiffusionDrive, LTF) rise to second and third. The two orderings therefore select
different policies.
In these scenes the no-collision gate carries \NumNCshareClose\% of the spread, while over all 783
scenes it carries \NumNCshare\% and the two map-compliance gates carry \NumMapShare\% (drivable area
\NumDacShare\%, driving direction \NumDdcShare\%): the leaderboard ranks these policies by map
compliance, and none of the exams above is visible in it. Of our exams, specificity predicts the
near-pedestrian ordering ($\rho=+0.66$), while hazard sensitivity does not ($+0.14$), because it is near zero for every policy on these frames.

\paragraph{Pedestrian TTC across driving sides}
Following the plan against the pedestrian's logged future, we count the moving-ego scenes in which the
minimum time to collision falls below 1.5\,s. Near-pedestrian scenes from the left-hand-drive cities
(nuScenes Boston and NAVSIM Las Vegas, Boston, Pittsburgh) and from Singapore give orderings that agree
at $\rho=\NumRhoTTC$. Both orderings coincide with planned distance ($\rho=\NumRhoTTCExpL$ and $\NumRhoTTCExpR$ with exposure).
NAVSIM's own TTC term, which counts all objects over 4\,s, agrees only weakly ($0.31$--$0.54$).

\paragraph{Which exam travels}
This is the question a deployment decision has to answer. The target is the ordering of the six
policies on the Singapore near-pedestrian scenes (original images), by TTC violations, clearance to
the pedestrian, pedestrian collisions and EPDMS. Each predictor is an ordering obtained without that data. The standard evaluations do not predict it: nuScenes L2 and collision rates, and the NAVSIM
leaderboard score, correlate with it at $-0.54$ to $+0.49$. The same pedestrian-centred readouts taken
on Boston or on the US cities do ($0.83$--$1.00$), and so does the exposure exam ($0.94$). Near-pedestrian EPDMS is predicted best by near-pedestrian EPDMS elsewhere ($0.89$) and by specificity
($0.77$). Hazard sensitivity and lighting invariance measure something else and do not predict these
orderings. Of the five exams, the one whose ordering best matches the new side's rate of sub-1.5\,s
time-to-proximity is not the one that measures how \emph{much} a policy reacts but the one that
measures how \emph{steadily} it drives: over the six policies and the \NumSGscenes-scene pool of
\cref{sec:axes}, specificity gives $\rho={\NumSpBeh}$, against ${\NumExpBeh}$ for exposure and
${\NumCfrBeh}$ for $\CFR$. This exam was not pre-registered, and we report it as exploratory. Cross-policy correlations rest on six policies, and we read them as orderings, not as tests. Specificity
asks how far a plan moves when an \emph{irrelevant} part of the scene is edited. A policy that answers
``not at all'' drives steadily, and steadiness is what survives the change of side. The decomposition
of \cref{sec:axes} says why: displacement that is not aimed away from the pedestrian is not
protection, and a policy producing a lot of it comes close to the pedestrian on both sides. Where a
standard metric measures what an exam measures, they agree (pedestrian collisions at $\rho=+0.94$, TTC and exposure). None shows whether the pedestrian changed the plan, and the full-set score hides the
near-pedestrian scenes. A rank does not carry the near-pedestrian ordering to a new site. The exposure and specificity exams, read from the same frames, do.

\paragraph{What a verdict costs}
For every exam and policy we subsample $n$ of the \NumPool{} frames that all six policies share, 400
times, and fit the spread of the estimate with $\mathrm{sd}(n)=a\sqrt{1/n-1/M}$, $M=\NumPool$ (\cref{fig:sample}).

\paragraph{Well-fitting exams}
Exposure, the lighting ratio and specificity obey this law ($R^2=0.97$--$1.00$). The fitted $a$
converts directly into the number of frames a verdict needs.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Lighting outweighs the pedestrian} ($\CFR<1$) is settled for every policy within
      \NumNstarCFRhi{} frames.
\item \emph{Exposure differs from the human's} is settled within \NumNstarExpHi{} frames for the four
      policies whose exposure is far from one. For AutoVLA and Alpamayo-1.5 it would take hundreds to
      thousands of frames, which is itself a finding, since they plan a human-like distance.
\item \emph{Orderings} of the six policies stabilise quickly: exposure with 10 frames
      (\NumRankExpTen\% of subsamples reach $\rho\ge0.6$ with the full ordering) and specificity with
      40 (\NumRankSPforty\%).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Poorly fitting exams}
Hazard sensitivity behaves differently. Its fits are poor ($R^2$ as low as 0.26) wherever few units need a reaction. Deciding that $\HS\neq0$ needs
57--1{,}326 frames, except for SimLingo, whose estimate sits on the threshold, and the ordering reaches \NumRankHSninety\% stability only at 90 frames.

In wall-clock terms, a 20\,s scene in this corpus yields about \NumFrPerScene{} usable
near-pedestrian frames, so the lighting verdict costs roughly \NumScCFR{} scenes and the exposure
verdict \NumScExp. Deciding that hazard sensitivity differs from zero costs \NumScHS---about
\NumMinHS{} minutes of logged driving that actually contains a pedestrian near the corridor.

The division is therefore clear. A few dozen frames diagnose exposure, specificity and lighting. Whether a policy manages hazard needs hundreds of frames that contain a genuine need to react.

\paragraph{What a small sample in the new domain buys}
We draw $k$ scenes at random from the NAVSIM pool of \cref{sec:axes}, re-measure $F$ and $I$ on them and compare against the
large-sample values, 200 draws per $k$. Ordering and level behave very differently. The ordering of
how often the policies come close---the share of scenes whose minimum time-to-proximity falls below
$1.5$\,s---is recovered almost immediately: $\rho={\NumBehRhoTen}$ at $k=10$ and $\rho={\NumBehRhoTwenty}$
at $k=20$ ($\rho\ge0.6$ in \NumBehHitTwenty\% of draws), rising to $\rho={\NumBehRhoForty}$ at $k=40$.
The level is not: the same $k=20$ sample misstates the violation rate by \NumBehErrTwenty{} points and
$\CFR$ by $\NumCfrErrTwenty$, and the rule hit-rates sit in between, at \NumPFErrTwenty{} points. Importing
the Boston $\CFR$ values instead misstates the Singapore ones by $\NumLhdCfrErr$, already worse than
re-measuring $k=40$ fresh scenes ($\NumCfrErrForty$), while 80 scenes reach $\NumCfrErrBig$ and 160
$\NumCfrErrHuge$. Values measured elsewhere are worth about forty scenes measured here.
