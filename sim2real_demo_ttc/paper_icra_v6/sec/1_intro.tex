\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
A leaderboard rank does not tell you how a driving policy will behave in a new domain. End-to-end and vision-language-action (VLA) driving policies are compared by benchmark scores, and a deployment decision is, implicitly, a bet that the rank those scores produce will still hold once the policy is driving at the new site, not just on someone else's roads. 
On scenes where a pedestrian is near the ego corridor, that bet fails for the six released policies we test: their rank on nuScenes'~\cite{caesar2020nuscenes} open-loop metrics or on NAVSIM's~\cite{caesar2021nuplan,dauner2024navsim} leaderboard score does not carry over to the deployment site ($\rho=-0.54$ to $+0.49$; \cref{sec:bench}), a gap that open-loop scores are known to open~\cite{zhao2026bridgesim,li2024egostatus,codevilla2019exploring}. The reason is not noise but mechanism. A score is an outcome measure, and an outcome can be right for the wrong reason: a policy that never approaches a pedestrian because it plans short trajectories scores as well as one that sees the pedestrian and yields, and the two behave very differently the first time the scene changes. Telling them apart means asking why the score came out as it did, usually from little data, because a closed-loop simulator for the target domain or a fleet test is exactly what the decision is meant to save.

However, no comprehensive evaluation method yet measures systematically how a policy will perform in
the target domain, so organisations deploying a model across domains fall back on extensive testing
and post-training in the deployment setting itself---rider-only fleets accumulating tens of millions
of miles before a safety claim can be made~\cite{kusano2025waymo56m}, and a generalist robot policy
improved through reinforcement learning on its own deployments and on-robot data
collection~\cite{pi2025pistar06}---a loop whose cost, measured in real-world
miles, is prohibitive~\cite{kalra2016driving}. This makes selecting a model for cross-domain
deployment harder still.

A considerable body of cross-domain work has been proposed---domain
adaptation~\cite{ganin2015dann}, domain generalisation~\cite{gulrajani2021lost,arjovsky2019irm} and
the like---but it concentrates on identifying the problem and on solving it through training, and
lacks any systematic assessment of how a policy behaves in the target domain. It therefore does not
substantively address the selection problem that arises at deployment.

Closer to our setting, \cite{kachaev2025don} improves a VLA's out-of-distribution generalisation by
aligning its visual representations, but it presumes an already-chosen vision--language model and
offers no procedure for selecting among policies, vision-only ones included. Representation
engineering~\cite{zou2023repe} locates and steers concepts in a language model's activations, yet it
is built around semantic prompts and does not carry over to policies whose input is images. Function
vectors~\cite{todd2024functionvectorslargelanguage} show that a task can be read out of, and
transferred through, a language model's hidden states; the same instruments have no counterpart for
the cross-domain evaluation of physical-AI policies. A systematic method for that evaluation is still
missing.


To address this, we propose the \emph{Check-Up}, a counterfactual diagnosis in three stages
(\cref{fig:method}). (a)~We construct pixel-level paired data: every logged frame is queried with and
without the target pedestrian and under a night-style photometric perturbation at two levels, and an
independent detector verifies every edit. (b)~From each pair we distil two axes of causal
attribution---the \emph{faithfulness} displacement $F$, the plan change caused by the pedestrian
itself, and the \emph{interference} displacement $I$, the change the same policy produces under an
edit that carries no driving-relevant information~\eqref{eq:FI}. Five exams, each with a control and
a normal range, are built on these paired plans and axes (\cref{tab:exams}): how far the policy plans
to drive, whether seeing the pedestrian buys safety, whether that benefit scales with the hazard,
whether the plan moves when nothing requires it, and how much the irrelevant edit moves it. (c)~Read
on one driving side and tested on the other, the exams yield a prognosis for the target domain: the
exam that transfers predicts the new side's near-miss rate at $\rho={\NumSpBeh}$, where the standard
scores reach only $-0.54$--$+0.49$, and each verdict can be priced in frames before it is used.

The contribution is the Check-Up, a counterfactual diagnosis of end-to-end and VLA driving
policies under domain shift, in three parts.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \emph{Pixel-level paired data.} Every logged frame is queried with and without the target
      pedestrian and under a night-style perturbation at two levels, each edit verified by an
      independent detector; six released policies are read on two driving sides.
\item \emph{Two causal axes.} A response is read as the faithfulness displacement $F$ and the
      interference displacement $I$~\eqref{eq:FI}, and counts as avoidance only if it moves, exceeds
      the jitter, and buys distance. Where the pedestrian lies on the planned path, \NumCfRealPct\%
      of responses qualify, and the lighting edit moves every plan further than the pedestrian does.
\item \emph{A priced prognosis.} Diagnosed on one driving side and tested on the other, orderings
      and verdicts transfer: the exam that transfers predicts the new side's near-miss rate at
      $\rho={\NumSpBeh}$, against $-0.54$--$+0.49$ for the standard scores, and each verdict is priced
      in frames---lighting within \NumNstarCFRhi, exposure within \NumNstarExpHi.
\end{enumerate}
