\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

End-to-end driving policies are compared by benchmark scores. A score summarises what a policy's
planned trajectories achieved on logged scenes: no collision, staying in the drivable area, making
progress~\cite{dauner2024navsim,cao2025pseudosim}. A score of this kind is an outcome measure, and an
outcome can be right for the wrong reason. A policy that never approaches a pedestrian because it
plans short trajectories scores as well as one that sees the pedestrian and yields, and the two
behave very differently the first time the scene changes. Deciding whether to deploy a policy in a new
city requires knowing which of the two it is, and it usually has to be decided from little data:
a closed-loop simulator for the target domain, or a fleet test, is exactly what the decision is
meant to save.

We propose a \emph{check-up}: a short list of exams, each with a control and a known normal range,
whose results are read as a diagnosis rather than a ranking (\cref{tab:exams}). Every exam is a
counterfactual edit of real frames. We remove the pedestrian from the ego corridor, or re-render the
frame at night, and read the change in the planned trajectory; an independent detector verifies every
edit. The central exam is a \emph{hazard-sensitivity curve}, measuring how much farther the whole
planned trajectory stays from the pedestrian's logged future because the policy can see the
pedestrian, against a model-independent hazard level. A specificity term applies a minimal-change
principle: when no reaction is needed, the plan should not move.

\paragraph{Displacement is not avoidance}
The quantity an edit yields most directly---how far the plan moves when the pedestrian is
removed---is a poor safety signal on its own, and treating it as one inverts the ranking. We
therefore decompose it into three parts: the magnitude of the response, the displacement the same
policy produces under an edit that has nothing to do with the pedestrian (its own jitter floor), and
the separation the response actually buys. On \NumSGscenes{} near-pedestrian scenes, the pedestrian
outweighs the irrelevant edit in only \NumFgtIlo--\NumFgtIhi\% of scenes; among the cells where the
plan did move, \NumMoveFar{} moved away from the pedestrian and \NumMoveNear{} moved towards it. The
asymmetry that survives is one-directional and useful: a near-zero response certifies that the
pedestrian did not enter the decision (\NumPFbig\% of scenes), while a large response certifies
nothing.

\paragraph{What the six policies do}
Applied to six end-to-end policies, the check-up separates quantities a benchmark score merges
(\cref{tab:report}). Their plans cover \NumExpLo--\NumExpHi{} times the distance the human drove.
Seeing the pedestrian improves the plan by \NumHSlo--\NumHShi{} on a $[-1,1]$ scale, and in five of
six policies the improvement does not grow with the hazard. Collisions with the pedestrian's logged
future therefore follow how far a policy plans to drive, not what it perceives. A night rendering
moves every policy more than the pedestrian does. We look inside the policies to explain this,
although we do not attempt a repair: the pedestrian leaves a trace in the features but is not decoded
by the policies' own semantic heads, the planner reads a grid too coarse to locate it, and its plan
ends before the pedestrian.

\paragraph{What a small check-up can support}
A check-up is only useful if it is cheap and if its operating range is known. We fit how each
estimate's spread shrinks with the number of frames: the lighting verdict is settled within
\NumNstarCFRhi{} frames and exposure within \NumNstarExpHi, while hazard sensitivity and the
direction of the lighting effect need hundreds. We then pre-registered seven predictions from a
diagnosis made on 88 Boston frames and tested them on Singapore. Orderings and verdicts transfer;
point values do not (\cref{tab:prereg}). Re-measuring the exams in the new city, the exam that
predicts behaviour there is not the one measuring how strongly a policy reacts but the one measuring
how steadily it drives ($\rho={\NumSpBeh}$ for specificity).

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item A small-sample check-up for driving policies: verified counterfactual edits, a hazard-sensitivity
      curve built on whole-trajectory separation, time to proximity and a minimal-change principle.
\item A decomposition of the counterfactual response into magnitude, jitter and separation, which
      turns a two-sided quantity into a one-sided certificate, together with a diagnosis of six
      policies and an explanation from inside the networks.
\item An account of what small data can and cannot establish: sample-size laws for every exam, a
      pre-registered cross-city transfer test, and a measurement of which exam predicts behaviour in
      a new city.
\end{enumerate}
