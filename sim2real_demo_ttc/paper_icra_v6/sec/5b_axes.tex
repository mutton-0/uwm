\section{Re-measuring $F$ and $I$ in the New Domain}
\label{sec:axes}

\input{tables/tab_axes}

\Cref{sec:sample} moved a \emph{diagnosis} from one driving side to the other. The more useful
question for deployment is whether the \emph{method} moves: can a handful of counterfactual cases in
the new domain characterise how the policies behave there? We repeat both interventions on the
\NumSGscenes{} NAVSIM Singapore near-pedestrian scenes and read, per scene, the two behavioural
displacements that correspond to $\hat v_F$ and $\hat v_I$ at the output: $F$, the plan displacement
caused by removing the target pedestrian, and $I$, that caused by the night rendering.

\paragraph{What $F$ and $I$ say per scene}
Across the ten detailed cases and six policies (\NumCaseCells{} cells), the median $F$ is \NumAxF\,m
and the median $I$ is \NumAxI\,m; the lighting moves the plan more in \NumAxShare\% of cells. Two rules follow from the axes. We state each in both
directions, so that every cell receives a prediction:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{P-F}: if $F<0.5$\,m, removing the pedestrian should leave the clearance and the contact
      outcome unchanged; if $F\ge0.5$\,m, it should change them.
\item \textbf{P-I}: if $\CFR<1$, the night rendering should move the outcome at least as much as the
      removal; if $\CFR\ge1$, the removal should move it at least as much as the night.
\end{itemize}
The two directions do not fare equally. On the detailed cases, P-F holds in \NumPFsmall/\NumPFsmallN{}
cells in the forward direction but only \NumPFrev/\NumPFrevN{} in the reverse; P-I holds in
\NumPIsmall/\NumPIsmallN{} and \NumPIrev/\NumPIrevN. Scaling both interventions to the full pool,
which all six policies cover, reproduces the asymmetry exactly (\cref{tab:axes}): P-F
holds in \NumPFbig\% of scenes forward against \NumPFbigRev\% reversed, while P-I holds in \NumPIbig\%
and \NumPIbigRev\%. A near-zero $F$ is therefore a reliable certificate that the pedestrian is not being
used, but a large $F$ is not a promise of safer behaviour.

\paragraph{When the plan does move, which way does it move?}
The reverse direction fails for an instructive reason: $F$ measures how far a plan moves, not where it
moves to. We therefore decompose it (\cref{fig:cases}e), reading $F$ against $I$---the same policy's
jitter floor under an unrelated edit---and against $\Delta S$, the change in clearance to the
pedestrian's logged future. A response counts as avoidance only if all three hold: the plan moved
($F\ge0.5$\,m), it moved more than the policy's own jitter ($F>I$), and the movement bought distance
($\Delta S\ge0.5$\,m).

Three readings follow. First, the pedestrian outweighs the irrelevant edit in only
\NumFgtIlo--\NumFgtIhi\% of scenes, so most of what $F$ registers sits inside the policy's own
variability---the pinched band of \cref{fig:cases}e. Second, only \NumRealN{} of \NumRealTot{} cells
(\NumRealPct\%) are genuine avoidance. Third, the displacement that does occur is not aimed: of the
\NumMoveN{} cells in which the plan moved, \NumMoveFar{} increased the clearance and \NumMoveNear{}
decreased it. Per policy the split differs sharply: DiffusionDrive reacts in few
scenes but in the right direction (median $\Delta S=\NumDDmed$\,m), while SimLingo moves in more
scenes than the other five combined and moves \emph{towards} the pedestrian more often than away
(\NumSLnear{} against \NumSLfar, median $\NumSLmed$\,m); it also has the highest rate of sub-1.5\,s
time-to-proximity (\cref{tab:axes}). Finally, what a policy yields does not grow with the hazard: the
rank correlation between $\Delta S$ and the deceleration the scene demands is
\NumRhoLo--\NumRhoHi{}.

An exam that rewarded responsiveness alone would therefore rank the least safe policy first.

\paragraph{What a small sample in the new domain buys}
We draw $k$ scenes at random from the pool, re-measure $F$ and $I$ on them and compare against the
large-sample values, 200 draws per $k$. Ordering and level behave very differently. The ordering of
how dangerously the policies behave---the share of scenes whose minimum time-to-proximity falls below
$1.5$\,s---is recovered almost immediately: $\rho={\NumBehRhoTen}$ at $k=10$ and $\rho={\NumBehRhoTwenty}$
at $k=20$ ($\rho\ge0.6$ in \NumBehHitTwenty\% of draws), rising to $\rho={\NumBehRhoForty}$ at $k=40$.
The level is not: the same $k=20$ sample misstates the violation rate by \NumBehErrTwenty{} points and
$\CFR$ by $\NumCfrErrTwenty$; the rule hit-rates sit in between, at \NumPFErrTwenty{} points. Importing
the Boston $\CFR$ values instead misstates the Singapore ones by $\NumLhdCfrErr$, already worse than
re-measuring $k=40$ fresh scenes ($\NumCfrErrForty$), while 80 scenes reach $\NumCfrErrBig$ and 160
$\NumCfrErrHuge$. Values measured elsewhere are worth about forty scenes measured here.

\paragraph{Which exam travels}
Of the exams in the report, the one that predicts the new domain's behaviour is not the one that
measures how \emph{much} a policy reacts, but the one that measures how \emph{steadily} it drives.
Correlating each Boston exam against the Singapore rate of sub-1.5\,s time-to-proximity over the six
policies, specificity gives $\rho={\NumSpBeh}$---the only value significant at the two-sided 5\% level
for $n{=}6$---against ${\NumExpBeh}$ for exposure and ${\NumCfrBeh}$ for $\CFR$. Specificity asks how far a plan moves when an
\emph{irrelevant} part of the scene is edited; a policy that answers ``not at all'' drives steadily,
and steadiness is what survives the change of side. (Exposure travels too: on the larger
original-image set of \cref{sec:bench} it reaches $0.94$ against the same target. Specificity is the
stronger predictor and the only one that clears significance here.) The decomposition above says why:
displacement that is not aimed away from the pedestrian is not protection, and a policy producing a
lot of it is dangerous on both sides.

\paragraph{What transfers and what must be re-measured}
The verdict $\CFR<1$ holds on both sides for every policy, but the value drifts by a factor of $0.6$
to $1.7$ and its across-policy ordering barely agrees between sides ($\rho={+}0.37$, against
${+}0.83$ for specificity); per-scene medians are weaker still (\cref{tab:axes}). The
\emph{instrument} transfers while its \emph{readings} do not.
