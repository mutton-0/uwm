\section{Re-measuring the Axes in the New Domain}
\label{sec:axes}

\input{tables/tab_axes}

The transfer test of \cref{sec:sample} moved a \emph{diagnosis} between cities. A more useful question
for deployment is whether the \emph{method} moves: can a handful of counterfactual cases in the new
domain characterise how the policies behave there? We repeat both interventions on the
\NumSGscenes{} NAVSIM Singapore near-pedestrian scenes, on the original benchmark images, and read the
two axes per scene:
$F$, the plan displacement caused by removing the target pedestrian, and $I$, the displacement caused
by the night rendering.

\paragraph{What the axes say per scene}
Across the ten detailed cases and six policies (\NumCaseCells{} cells), the median $F$ is \NumAxF\,m
and the median $I$ is \NumAxI\,m; the lighting moves the plan more in \NumAxShare\% of cells. Two rules
follow directly from the axes. We state each in both directions, so that every cell receives a
prediction rather than only those whose premise happens to hold:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{P-F}: if $F<0.5$\,m, removing the pedestrian should leave the clearance and the contact
      outcome unchanged; if $F\ge0.5$\,m, it should change them.
\item \textbf{P-I}: if $\CFR<1$, the night rendering should move the outcome at least as much as the
      removal; if $\CFR\ge1$, the removal should move it at least as much as the night.
\end{itemize}
The two directions do not fare equally. On the detailed cases, P-F holds in \NumPFsmall/\NumPFsmallN{}
cells in the forward direction but only \NumPFrev/\NumPFrevN{} in the reverse; P-I holds in
\NumPIsmall/\NumPIsmallN{} and \NumPIrev/\NumPIrevN. Scaling both interventions to the full pool,
which all six policies cover, reproduces the asymmetry exactly (\cref{tab:axes}): P-F
holds in \NumPFbig\% of scenes forward against \NumPFbigRev\% reversed, while P-I holds in \NumPIbig\%
and \NumPIbigRev\%. A near-zero $F$ axis is therefore a reliable certificate that the pedestrian is not
being used, but a large $F$ is not a promise of safer behaviour.

\paragraph{When the plan does move, which way does it move?}
The reverse direction fails for a specific and instructive reason. $F$ measures how far a plan moves,
not where it moves to, so we decompose it (\cref{tab:decomp}). Alongside $F$ we read $I$, the
displacement the same policy produces under an edit that has nothing to do with the pedestrian, which
serves as its own jitter floor, and $\Delta S$, the change in clearance to the pedestrian's logged
future. A response deserves to be called avoidance only if it satisfies all three conditions: the plan
moved ($F\ge0.5$\,m), it moved more than the policy's own jitter ($F>I$), and the movement bought
distance ($\Delta S\ge0.5$\,m).

Three readings follow. First, the pedestrian outweighs the irrelevant edit in only
\NumFgtIlo--\NumFgtIhi\% of scenes, so most of what $F$ registers sits inside the policy's own
variability. Second, of the \NumRealTot{} policy--scene cells, \NumRealN{} (\NumRealPct\%) are genuine
avoidance by the definition above. Third, the displacement that does occur is not aimed: of the
\NumMoveN{} cells in which the plan moved, \NumMoveFar{} increased the clearance, \NumMoveNear{}
decreased it, and \NumMoveFlat{} left it unchanged. Per policy the split is sharply different.
DiffusionDrive reacts in few scenes but in the right direction (\NumDDfar{} away against
\NumDDnear{} towards, median $\Delta S=\NumDDmed$\,m). SimLingo moves in more scenes than the other
five combined and moves \emph{towards} the pedestrian more often than away (\NumSLnear{} against
\NumSLfar, median $\NumSLmed$\,m); it is also the policy with the highest rate of sub-1.5\,s
time-to-proximity (\cref{tab:axes}). Finally, the clearance a policy yields does not grow with the
hazard: the rank correlation between $\Delta S$ and the deceleration the scene demands is
\NumRhoLo--\NumRhoHi{} across the six policies.

An exam that rewarded responsiveness alone would therefore rank the least safe policy first. This is
the asymmetry we want from a check-up: it can clear a policy of using the pedestrian, and it refuses to
credit a policy merely for reacting.

\paragraph{A small sample in the new domain ranks; it does not calibrate}
We can now ask what a handful of new-domain cases actually buys. We draw $k$ scenes at random from the
shared pool, re-measure both axes on them, and compare the resulting estimate to the large-sample
values, 200 draws per $k$. Two things behave very differently. The \emph{ordering} of how dangerously
the policies behave---the share of scenes whose minimum time-to-proximity falls below
$1.5$\,s---is recovered almost immediately: $\rho={\NumBehRhoTen}$ at $k=10$ and $\rho={\NumBehRhoTwenty}$
at $k=20$ (with $\rho\ge0.6$ in \NumBehHitTwenty\% of draws), rising to $\rho={\NumBehRhoForty}$ at $k=40$.
The \emph{level} is not: the same $k=20$ sample still misstates the violation rate by \NumBehErrTwenty{}
points on average, and $\CFR$ by $\NumCfrErrTwenty$. The rule hit-rates sit in between; $k=20$ estimates
them to \NumPFErrTwenty{} points. A small check-up in a new domain therefore supports a ranking and a
verdict, not a calibrated number---which is exactly the use we propose for it.

\paragraph{Re-measuring beats importing}
Importing the Boston $\CFR$ values misstates the Singapore ones by $\NumLhdCfrErr$ on average, which
is already worse than re-measuring $k=40$ fresh scenes in the new city ($\NumCfrErrForty$); 80 scenes
bring the error to $\NumCfrErrBig$ and 160 to $\NumCfrErrHuge$. Values measured elsewhere are worth
about forty scenes measured here.

\paragraph{Which exam travels}
Of the exams in the report, the one that predicts the new domain's behaviour is not the one that
measures how \emph{much} a policy reacts, but the one that measures how \emph{steadily} it drives.
Correlating each Boston exam against the Singapore rate of sub-1.5\,s time-to-proximity over the six
policies---a comparison that crosses the recording as well as the city (\cref{sec:checkup})---specificity gives $\rho={\NumSpBeh}$---the only value significant at the two-sided 5\% level
for $n{=}6$---against ${\NumExpBeh}$ for exposure and ${\NumCfrBeh}$ for $\CFR$. Specificity is the
exam that asks how far a plan moves when an \emph{irrelevant} part of the scene is edited; a policy
that answers ``not at all'' is one whose trajectory is stable, and stability is what carries across
cities. The ordering used here is the one measured on the counterfactual pool, whose readout excludes
the scenes that carry no removal edit; on the larger original-image set of \cref{sec:bench} exposure
reaches $0.94$ against this same target. Exposure therefore travels on both pools, but specificity is
the stronger predictor and the only one that clears significance here. This is consistent with the decomposition above: displacement that is not aimed away from the
pedestrian is not protection, and a policy that produces a lot of it is dangerous in both cities.

\paragraph{What transfers and what must be re-measured}
The verdict $\CFR<1$ holds on both driving sides for every policy, but the value drifts by a factor of
$0.6$ to $1.7$, and the across-policy ordering of $\CFR$ barely agrees between sides ($\rho={+}0.37$,
against ${+}0.83$ for specificity). A single scene is weaker still: the per-scene medians
(\cref{tab:axes}) sit well below either aggregate. The practical reading is that the \emph{instrument}
transfers while its \emph{readings} do not: entering a new domain, one re-measures the axes there with
a few dozen scenes rather than importing numbers measured elsewhere.
