\begin{abstract}
A policy's rank on nuScenes' open-loop metrics or NAVSIM's leaderboard score predicts its rank at a
new deployment site only about as well as a coin flip ($\rho=-0.54$--$+0.49$ on near-pedestrian
scenes): a benchmark score reports an outcome, not the behaviour behind it. When a vulnerable road
user calls for a reaction, the score does not say whether that road user had any part in the outcome,
so a ranking cannot separate a policy that yields from one that merely never gets close. We propose a \emph{check-up} --- a few hundred verified, pixel-level counterfactual edits
(removing the pedestrian, re-rendering the scene at night) read as a diagnosis rather than a ranking.
The edits reduce each scene to two axes: the displacement the pedestrian causes, and the displacement
an irrelevant edit causes. Raw magnitude is then a poor safety signal. Replaying \NumSGscenes{}
right-hand-drive scenes at input speeds of 2--8\,m/s, so that the pedestrian does lie on the planned
path, only \NumCfRealPct\% of the responses that are called for qualify as genuine avoidance on a
three-part test; on those cells neither the clearance to the pedestrian nor the planned distance
moves. We pre-register a diagnosis made on 88 left-hand-drive
frames and test it after the switch to right-hand drive: orderings transfer while point values do not,
and the exam measuring how \emph{steadily} a policy drives predicts the new side's near-miss rate at
$\rho={\NumSpBeh}$ --- a target none of the standard evaluations predicts. A check-up therefore prices
a deployment verdict in tens of frames rather than in a new benchmark.
\end{abstract}
